⚡ current chapter

Quiet work, real stakes.

Lately I'm focused on the kind of product work that doesn't get a launch tweet — the systems that live behind the experience and only get noticed when they're not working. A few things I've come to believe doing this kind of work:

Signal over noise.

Most of what I do right now lives behind the product, not in front of it. The work is about reading patterns in large amounts of behavioural data — figuring out which patterns are signal, which are noise, and which ones quietly warn you about something before it becomes a problem.

Decisions, not just dashboards.

Insights are interesting; decisions are what move the metric. A lot of the job is making sure the signals we generate flow into the systems and people that actually act on them — instead of sitting on a dashboard nobody opens.

Tradeoffs you can't optimise cleanly.

This kind of work is full of tradeoffs without clean answers — speed vs. precision, automation vs. judgment, the cost of being wrong in one direction vs. the other. The job is to name the tradeoff honestly, take a position, and revisit it when the world changes.

Two timescales, at once.

The systems improve incrementally, but have to hold up in real time. So I'm always thinking in two timescales — what we ship this sprint, and what we'll wish we'd built six months ago.

More on capabilities available on request — happy to walk through specifics in conversation.

Recognition details shared happily, one-on-one — ask away.

// the teacher who never quite left

At some point in school I wanted to be a teacher, and I never fully let that go. It shows up in meetings, in the way I write PRDs, in the unconscious "okay, in plain language..." I reach for before explaining something. Explaining things forces you to actually understand them — and the places where the explanation gets clumsy are usually where the real product work is hiding.

// a long memory for kindness

I notice when people are generous with their time. Guidance compounds in a way money doesn't, and the people who've taken time to teach me something stay bookmarked in my head for a long time. I try to be that person for the people working alongside me — sometimes formally, mostly not.
